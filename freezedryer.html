💡 What you are seeing

The product (vials on shelves) is frozen to −40°C to −80°C. A vacuum is applied. The shelf temperature is slowly raised — but because pressure is far below the triple point of water, the ice sublimates directly to vapour (skips the liquid phase). Water vapour travels to the condenser (−60°C) where it freezes onto the condenser coils. The product stays frozen and dry.

🔑 Freeze drying = sublimation of ice under vacuum. Ice → vapour (no liquid water ever formed). This is the only drying method that removes water WITHOUT heating the product above 0°C — making it ideal for proteins, vaccines, and heat-labile biologics.

Principle — Sublimation Under Vacuum

Water exists as solid, liquid, or gas depending on temperature and pressure. At the triple point (0.01°C, 6.11 mbar), all three phases coexist. Below the triple point pressure, ice sublimates directly to vapour without forming liquid water. Freeze drying exploits this: reduce pressure below 0.1–1 mbar → ice sublimates → water removed without thawing the product → product structure preserved.

Three Stages of Freeze Drying

  • Stage 1 — Freezing: Product cooled to −40 to −80°C; water crystallises as ice; product solidifies; nucleation controlled to produce optimal ice crystal size
  • Stage 2 — Primary drying (sublimation): Vacuum applied (0.1–1 mbar); shelf temperature raised to −20 to +20°C; free ice sublimates; longest stage (12–48 hours)
  • Stage 3 — Secondary drying (desorption): Shelf temperature raised to +20–40°C; bound water (adsorbed water) removed; final moisture content < 1–3%
🔑 Product temperature must remain below the collapse temperature (Tc) throughout primary drying. Above Tc, the dried cake loses structure (collapses). Excipients (sucrose, mannitol, glycine) raise Tc — allowing higher shelf temperature → faster primary drying.

Pharmaceutical Applications

  • Protein biologics — monoclonal antibodies, enzymes, growth factors
  • Vaccines — viral and bacterial vaccines requiring long shelf life
  • Peptides and small molecule heat-sensitive APIs
  • Blood products — plasma proteins, coagulation factors
  • Diagnostic reagents — immunoassay kits, PCR reagents
Q1. In freeze drying, ice is removed by the process of:
✅ Sublimation below triple point: triple point of water = 0.01°C, 6.11 mbar. In freeze dryer: pressure reduced to 0.1–1 mbar (below triple point) → thermodynamic phase diagram dictates: at this pressure, solid ice converts directly to vapour (no liquid phase possible). The vacuum drives the partial pressure of water vapour to near zero → strong thermodynamic driving force for ice sublimation. Condenser at −60°C traps vapour as ice → maintaining low vapour pressure throughout the chamber.
Q2. Lyophilisation (freeze drying) is chosen over spray drying for protein biologic formulations because:
✅ Protein stability comparison: Spray drying — inlet 150–250°C; outlet 60–80°C — most proteins denature at 60–80°C → aggregation, loss of activity → unacceptable for biologic drugs. Freeze drying — primary drying typically −20 to 0°C; secondary drying 20–40°C — well within protein stability range → structure preserved → activity maintained. Exception: some robust proteins (e.g., BSA, immunoglobulins) CAN be spray-dried with protective excipients. For most therapeutic proteins (mAbs, enzymes, vaccines): freeze drying is mandatory.
Q3. The purpose of adding sucrose or trehalose to a protein formulation before freeze drying is:
✅ Lyoprotection mechanism: (1) Water replacement hypothesis — during drying, water molecules hydrogen-bonded to protein surface are removed → without replacement, protein unfolds (denatures). Sucrose/trehalose form H-bonds with same surface groups → replace water → protein structure maintained. (2) Vitrification — sugars form amorphous glass matrix (high Tg) encapsulating protein → immobilises protein molecules → prevents aggregation during storage. Sucrose preferred for most proteins; trehalose for membranes and some enzymes.
