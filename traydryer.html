💡 What you are seeing

Hot air enters from the left (red arrows) at 40–80°C. It flows across each tray, picks up moisture from the wet granules, and exits as humid air on the right (grey arrows). Granules change colour from blue (wet) to orange (dry) as moisture evaporates. Air circulation is assisted by a fan.

🔑 Tray dryer dries from outside-in. The surface dries first and can form a hard crust (case hardening) — trapping moisture inside. This is why drying takes 8–24 hours and why Fluid Bed Dryer is faster.

Principle

The Tray Dryer removes moisture from pharmaceutical materials (wet granules, crude drugs, chemical intermediates) by passing hot air over trays containing the material. Moisture evaporates from the surface of the material into the air stream and is carried out through the exhaust. Heat transfer is by convection from the hot air to the wet material surface.

Construction

  • Cabinet: Insulated stainless steel cabinet; airtight doors with seals; size: 1–5 m long
  • Trays: 10–20 perforated SS trays on sliding rails; tray depth 2–5 cm; material spread evenly in thin layer
  • Heating element: Steam coils or electric heaters at air inlet; heats air to 40–80°C
  • Fan / blower: Circulates hot air across trays at controlled velocity (0.5–5 m/s); ensures uniform temperature distribution
  • Air dampers: Control ratio of recirculated air to fresh air (typically 80–90% recirculation for energy efficiency)
  • Temperature controller: Thermostat maintains inlet air temperature; outlet temperature monitored to detect drying endpoint

Drying Stages

  • Constant rate period: Surface of granules is wet; evaporation rate = constant; temperature of material = wet bulb temperature. Rate controlled by air velocity and temperature
  • Falling rate period: Surface begins to dry; moisture must migrate from centre to surface; rate decreases; material temperature rises toward dry bulb temperature
  • Endpoint: Loss on Drying (LOD) sampled hourly; target typically 1–3% moisture for granules
🔑 Drying endpoint monitoring: outlet air temperature rises when drying is complete (no more evaporative cooling). Take LOD samples every 30–60 minutes; stop drying when LOD = specification (e.g., NMT 2.0%).

Pharmaceutical Applications

  • Drying wet granules after wet granulation (before tablet compression)
  • Drying crude drugs (plant material) before extraction or milling
  • Drying chemical intermediates and APIs after crystallisation or precipitation
  • Drying moisture-sensitive excipients before use

Advantages and Disadvantages

  • ✅ Simple construction — low capital cost
  • ✅ Flexible — handles large pieces, sticky materials, fragile granules
  • ✅ Easy to load and unload different materials
  • ✅ Suitable for small batches and pilot scale
  • ❌ Slow — 8 to 24 hours typical drying time
  • ❌ Non-uniform drying — outside surface dries faster than centre (case hardening)
  • ❌ Labour intensive — manual loading, unloading, and turning of granules
  • ❌ Cross-contamination risk — open trays during loading/unloading
  • ❌ Poor energy efficiency (large cabinet volume to heat)
Q1. The phenomenon of "case hardening" in a Tray Dryer means:
✅ Case hardening: rapid surface evaporation → dry hard shell forms → moisture inside cannot escape → LOD appears acceptable on surface but moisture trapped inside → during storage, internal moisture migrates outward → tablet problems (sticking, picking, chemical degradation). Prevention: lower inlet temperature; higher air velocity (faster moisture removal from surface); spreading granules in thin layer; turning/stirring granules during drying. Fluid Bed Dryer avoids this entirely — each particle individually exposed.
Q2. During tray drying, the outlet air temperature stays constant initially then rises sharply. The sharp rise indicates:
✅ Drying endpoint by outlet temperature: while wet granules are present → evaporation absorbs heat energy → outlet air temperature = wet bulb temperature (constant). When granules are dry → no evaporation → no evaporative cooling → outlet temperature rises toward inlet temperature. GMP practice: monitor outlet temperature continuously; confirm endpoint with LOD test on representative tray samples. Temperature rise = signal to test LOD.
Q3. The Tray Dryer uses 80–90% air recirculation rather than 100% fresh air because:
✅ Air recirculation economics: heating fresh air from ambient (25°C) to 60°C requires significant energy. Recirculating 80–90% of already-hot exhaust air saves heating cost. The 10–20% fresh air addition dilutes humid exhaust air — maintaining the humidity gradient (driving force for evaporation). If 100% recirculation used, exhaust air becomes saturated (100% RH) → no humidity gradient → drying stops. Optimal balance: maximum recirculation while maintaining adequate drying rate.
Q4. Loss on Drying (LOD) test is performed on tray dryer output to:
✅ LOD test (IP/USP): weigh sample (1–5 g) → heat at specified temperature (usually 105°C for 30–60 min, or until constant weight) → re-weigh → LOD% = (mass loss / initial mass) × 100. For tablet granulations: LOD specification typically 1.5–3.0%. Too wet (> 3%): tablet sticking, mold risk, slow dissolution. Too dry (< 1%): brittle tablets, poor compressibility, static electricity problems. Modern tray dryers: in-line NIR probe measures LOD continuously — real-time monitoring without sampling.
